Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics for numeric variables

While performing descriptive analysis of numeric variables, it is essential to look at measure of central tendency and measure of dispersion. The measure of central tendency along with measure of dispersion parameters is fundamental in statistics and data analysis. They help us describe data distributions, assess data quality, detect outliers, and make informed decisions about data analysis techniques. By understanding both, we can gain a comprehensive view of your dataset’s characteristics, enabling you to draw meaningful insights and perform robust statistical analyses.

Measure of Central Tendency

It is the summary measure that attempts to describe the whole dataset with the single value that represents the middle or center of its distribution. The characteristics required for the ideal measure of central tendency are as follows:

  1. It should be simple, comprehensive to calculate and understand
  2. It should be based on all observations
  3. It should be least affected by extreme values or outliers
  4. It should be least affected by fluctation of sampling
  5. It should be suitable for comparision between groups

There are three measures of central tendency:

  • Mean

  • Median

  • Mode

Mean

There are two different types of mean - a) arithmetic mean and b) Geometric mean

Arithmetic mean

It is the mathematical average value of the set of data. It is calculated by summing up the observations, divided by number of observations. It represents the arithmetic center of the data and is sensitive to extreme values. Its characteristics are as follows:

  • It is the most popular and commonly used measure of central tendency.

  • Its value is affected by extreme values or outliers.

  • It can be used to compare between groups as it’s a single value

Formula

The formula for the arithmetic mean of a set of $ n $ numbers \(( x_1, x_2, \dots...., x_n )\) is given by:

\[mean = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n} \]

where,

\(mean\) is the arithmetic mean

\(n\) is the total number of observations

\(x_i\) represents each individual observation in the dataset

Geometric mean

The geometric mean of n numbers of data points is obtained by multiplying all the data points and taking the nth root of them. To calculate geometric mean, observations shoul be non-zero and non-negative. Geometric mean is not affected extreme values. It is very difficult to understand and calculate. It is used to describe data with extreme skewness to the right

Formula

The formula for the geometric mean of a set of \(n\) positive numbers \((x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)\) is given by:

\[ GM = \sqrt[n]{x_1 \cdot x_2 \cdot \dots \cdot x_n} = \left( \prod_{i=1}^n x_i \right)^{1/n} \]

Where

\(GM\) is the geometric mean

\(n\) is the total number of observations

\(x_i\) represents each individual observation in the dataset

Harmonic mean

The harmonic mean is another measure of central tendency that is useful when dealing with rates or ratios. It is the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocal of the set of non-zero data points.

Formula

The formula to calculate harmonic mean for grouped and ungrouped mean are as follows:

Ungrouped Data

The formula for the harmonic mean of a set of \(n\) numbers \((x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)\) is given by:

\[ HM = \frac{n}{\frac{1}{x_1} + \frac{1}{x_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{x_n}} \]

Where:

\(HM\) is the harmonic mean

\(n\) is the total number of observations

\(x_i\) represents each individual observation in the dataset

Grouped Data

For grouped data with frequencies \(( f_1, f_2, \dots, f_k )\) and corresponding values \(( x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k )\):

\[ HM = \frac{\sum f_i}{\sum \frac{f_i}{x_i}} \]

Where:

\(HM\) the harmonic mean

\(f_i\) is the frequency of the \(i^{th}\) value

\(x_i\) is the value of the \(i^{th}\) observation

Median

It is the middle most observation in data if data is arranged in ascending or descending order. It is simple to calculate but it is not as popular as mean. Its characteristics are:

  • It’s robust to outliers, making it a better choice when data is skewed or has extreme values.

  • It can be used to compare between groups as its a single value

Mode

It is the most frequently occurring value in the set of observations. There are chances of having multiple modes and no mode. Some characteistics of mode are as follows:

  • It can’t be used to compare between groups as it can have multiple possible values or no value

  • It is not popular as mean and median.

Measure of Dispersion

The values of variables varies with person, place and time. The height or weight of person differs between person. This variation is a biological variability. Measure of dispersion is the measure of how spread out of values in the dataset representing variability. It is also known as measure of variation.  Different measures of dispersion are as follows:

  • Range

  • Coefficient of range

  • Standard deviation

  • Variance

  • Coefficient of variance

  • Mean deviation from mean, median and mode

  • Quartile deviation and interquartile range

  • Percentile

Range

It is the basic form of measure of dispersion and defined as the difference between largest and smallest observation. If X and Y are the smallest and largest observation in the dataset respectively, the range,

\(R =Y-X\) where Y is highest value and X is lowest value

Example: For a data of BMI of 6 individuals: $17.1, 18.3, 19.3, 24.2, 21.2, 27.8 $

\(Range (R) = 27.8-17.1 = 10.7\)

Coefficient of Range

It is the ralative measure of corresponding to range. It can be used to compared variation between two different distributions. The formula to calculate coefficient of range is as follows:

\[ CoR= \frac{Y-X}{Y+X} \]where Y is highest value and X is lowest value

The coefficient of range for above example is as follows:

\(CoR =\frac{10.7}{44.9} = 0.24\)

where \(Y-X= 27.8-17.1\) and\(Y+X= 27.8+17.1\)

Standard Deviation

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance. It represents the average deviation of data points from the mean. A low standard deviation indicates that data points are close to the mean, while a high standard deviation suggests greater dispersion. The formula to calculate standar deviation is as follows:

\[ SD = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu)^2} \]

Where,

\(SF\): Standard deviation

\(x_i\): Individual data points

\(\mu\): Mean of the data

\(N\): Number of data points

Some pros and cons of standard deviation are as follows:

a. It resolves the problem of ignoring the sign on mean deviation by squaring the deviations.

b. It is based on all observations

c. It is useful in calculationg standard error. The formula to calculate standard error, \(SE=\frac{SD}{\sqrt{n}}\)

d. It gives greater weights to extreme values.

Variance

Variance measures the average of the squared differences from the mean. It quantifies how much data points deviate from the mean and provides valuable information about data dispersion.

The formula to calculate variance is as follows:

\[ Var = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu)^2 \]

Where:

\(Var\): Variance

\(x_i\): Individual data points

\(\mu\): Mean of the data

\(N\): Number of data points

Mean Deviation

It is the average of the deviations of values from the measure of central tendency ignoring the sign of these deviations.

Quartiles deviation and interquartile range

Quartiles are the three points in the dataset that divide it into four groups each containing 25% of the total data points.  If Q1, Q2, Q3 are the three quartiles then interquartile range (IQR) is Q3-Q1. Q2 is the median. Some characteristics of quartiles are as follows:

Coefficient of Variance

It is the ratio of standard deviation with respect to mean expressed as percentage. Lower the COV, lower is the variability in the dataset. Some characteristics of Coefficient of variance are as follows:

  • It is useful in comparing variability between two distributions

  • It is expressed in percent

  • It can be used to compared variation between two data in different units

The formula to calculate coefficient of variance is as follows:

\[ COV = \frac{SD}{Mean}*100 \]

Example: If the mean height of students in a class is 172.4 cm and standard deviation is 10.1cm. The COV is 10.1/172.4*100 = 5.86%. It means the standard deviation is 5.86% of its mean value.

Descriptive table for single variable

Frequency

We can use table() function to generate frequency table for categorical variables. Within the function table, you can have additional arguments like useNA which is used when you want to include NA in the table. The useNA argument takes three inputs "no", "ifany" and "always". The default is "no".

table(data$Sex)
#> 
#>    0    1 
#> 7440 4913
table(data$Sex, useNA="no")
#> 
#>    0    1 
#> 7440 4913
table(data$Sex, useNA="ifany") # Displays NA column in table if there are any missings within variable
#> 
#>    0    1 
#> 7440 4913
table(data$Sex, useNA="always") # Always displays NA column in the table even if missings don't exist
#> 
#>    0    1 <NA> 
#> 7440 4913    0

Proportion

To calculate the proportions, you can use prop.table() function which determines the proportion of each categories within the variable. The prop.table() function is always used with table() function for summarized table, otherwise the prop.table() command generates proportion for each observation of the variable.

sex <- table(data$Sex) # Generate table and save to a object here "sex"
prop.table(sex) # This will generate proportion
#> 
#>         0         1 
#> 0.6022828 0.3977172
# To calculate the percentage
prop.table(sex)*100
#> 
#>        0        1 
#> 60.22828 39.77172
# Round off the percentage
round(prop.table(sex)*100, 1) # Round function has 2 arguments, one is the value to round off (here: proportion table) and second is the number of digits after decimal for the value.
#> 
#>    0    1 
#> 60.2 39.8
# You can do this using piping (|>) "tidyverse" package is to be loaded before using piping
100*table(data$Sex) |> 
  prop.table() |> 
  round(3)
#> 
#>    0    1 
#> 60.2 39.8

Bar chart and Pie chart

Pie chart: For pie chart, you can use pie() function. To create pie chart, use table that you have generated earlier.

Usage: pie(x, labels = names(x), col = NULL, main = NULL, ...)

  • x: A numeric vector containing non-negative values, representing the sizes of the slices in the pie chart.
  • labels: (Optional) A vector of labels for the slices. By default, it uses the names of x.
  • col: (Optional) A vector of colors for the slices. If not specified, a default set of colors is used.
  • main: (Optional) The title for the pie chart.
  • ...: Additional graphical parameters (e.g., cex for character expansion, border for slice borders).
# Default pie chart
pie(sex)
# Add title to the pie chart
pie(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of population")
# Change labels from Male and Female to M and F respectively
pie(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of population",
    labels = c("F", "M"))
# Change color of slices 
pie(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of population", 
    labels = c("Female", "Male"),
    col = c("pink", "lightblue"))
# Change size of labels (using cex) and change border color
pie(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of population",
    labels = c("Female", "Male"),
    col = c("pink", "lightblue"),
    cex=2,# Changes the font size of the labels
    border = "white")

Bar chart: For bar chart, you can use barplot() function. To create bar chart, lets use the same table that we generated earlier.

Usage: barplot(height, names.arg, beside = TRUE, col = NULL, main = NULL, xlab = NULL, ylab = NULL)

  • height: A numeric vector or matrix containing the heights of the bars.
  • names.arg: (Optional) A vector of names to be plotted below each bar.
  • beside: (Optional) Logical. If TRUE, the bars will be plotted beside each other; if FALSE, bars will be plotted as stacked bars.
  • col: (Optional) A vector of colors for the bars.
  • main, xlab, ylab: (Optional) Titles for the main plot and the x and y-axes.
# Default barplot
barplot(sex)  
# Add title to bar chart
barplot(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of population")
# Change x-axis and y-axis labels
barplot(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of population",
    ylab="Frequency",
    xlab="Gender", beside = F)
# Change labels of the sex
barplot(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of sex",
    names.arg = row.names(sex), # you can manually change it as c("Female", "Male")
    ylab="Frequency",
    xlab="Gender", beside = F)
# Change color of bar 
barplot(sex,
    main="Sex-wise distribution of sex", 
    col = c("pink", "lightblue"),
    las = 1)

Frequency

Same as the frequency for single categorical variable, we can use the function table() but with two variables as arguments inside the table() function to create a cross-tabulation.

#>     Brahmin_chhetri Dalit Janajati Madheshi Others

# Using dplyr
library(dplyr)
data |> 
  select(Sex, Ethnicity) |> 
  table()

library(data.table)
data.dt <- data.table(data)

Now try the cross-tabulation for other variables and see how the results change.

Proportions

Same as proportion for single categorical variable, you can use prop.table() function to determine proportion for cross-tabulation. But in cross-tabulation, you will need additional argument if you wish to calculate row proportion or column proportion or overall proportion.

  • margin = 1: for row percent/proportion
  • margin = 2: for column percent/proportion

Descriptive statistics for numerical variables

The basic functions for the descriptive statistics of numerical variables and their use are as follows:

Functions Use
mean (x, na.rm=TRUE) Arithmetic mean
sd(x, na.rm=TRUE) Standard deviation
var(x, na.rm=TRUE) Variance
median (x, na.rm=T) Median
quantile(x, probs, type) Quantiles of x probs: with probabilities
min (x, na.rm=T) Minimum value
max(x, na.rm=T) Maximum value
range(x, na.rm=T) Minimum and maximum value

Descriptives of single variables

Measure of central tendency and dispersion

The mean, median, mode, quantile, standard deviations, variance minimum, maximum, range of the data can be calculated using several base functions like mean(), median(), sd(), var(), max(), min(), range(), etc.

# Mean
mean(data$Age, na.rm=T) 
#> [1] 29.84554
# Standard Deviation
sd(data$Age, na.rm=T) 
#> [1] 9.973959
# Variance
var(data$Age, na.rm=T) 
#> [1] 99.47987
# Median
median(data$Age, na.rm=T) 
#> [1] 29
# Percentiles
quantile(data$Age,probs=c(0.25, 0.5, 0.75), na.rm=T) 
#> 25% 50% 75% 
#>  21  29  38
# Minimum
min(data$Age, na.rm=T) 
#> [1] 15
# Maximum
max(data$Age, na.rm=T) 
#> [1] 49
# Range

Using base function (tapply and aggregate)

  • tapply is used to apply a function over subsets of a vector. It is particularly useful when you have a vector and want to apply a function to subsets of that vector based on some factor variable.

Skewness and Kurtosis

  • Skewness: Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution. It indicates whether the data is skewed to the left (negatively skewed) or to the right (positively skewed). A skewness value close to 0 suggest a roughly symmetric distribution. Negative skewness indicates a tail to the left while positive skewness indicates a tail to the right
  • Kurtosis: Kurtosis measures the “tailedness” of a distribution. It reflects how much data is in the tails and how sharp or flat the peak is. A kurtosis value around 0 is associated to normal distribution. Positive kurtosis indicates heavier tails while negative kurtosis indicates lighter tails.

We can calculate skewness and kurtosis using moments package. Lets calculate skewness and kurtosis in our dataset.

Function used: skewness () and kurtosis()

# install.packages("moments")
library(moments)

# skewness
skewness(data$Age) # Interpretation: slight right ward skew
#> [1] 0.245344
kurtosis(data$Age) # Interpretation: it is higher than 3 (this indicates heavier tails)
#> [1] 1.868973

Testing normality of numeric variables

Normality is an important assumption in many statistical tests, such as t-tests, ANOVA, linear regression, and parametric tests. Ensuring that data is normally distributed helps validate the results of these tests and ensures the reliability of statistical inferences. In the figure below, only middle one is normally distributed whereas other two are not.

Normality testing can be conducted using several methods:

  1. Statistical test
    1. Shapiro-Wilk test
    2. Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test
  2. Graphical
    1. Q-Q plot
    2. Histogram

Shapiro-Wilk Test

The Shapiro-Wilk test is a widely used statistical test for normality. It evaluates the null hypothesis that a sample comes from a normally distributed population. It is particularly effective for small to moderate sample sizes (typically < 50).

The test statistic, W, compares the observed data to what would be expected under normality. A p-value < 0.05 often leads to rejecting the null hypothesis, indicating non-normality.

dt <- rnorm(1000)

# Shapiro wilk test (works for sample <5000)
shapiro.test(dt)
#> 
#>  Shapiro-Wilk normality test
#> 
#> data:  dt
#> W = 0.99786, p-value = 0.2294

Interpretation: The test statistic (W) is 0.99786, and the p-value is 0.7844. With a high p-value, there is no significant evidence to reject the null hypothesis, suggesting that the data is reasonably consistent with a normal distribution.

Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test is a non-parametric test used to compare a sample distribution to a reference distribution (e.g., normal distribution). It measures the maximum distance between the empirical distribution function of the sample and the cumulative distribution function of the reference distribution. While it can be used for normality testing, it is less powerful than the Shapiro-Wilk test for this purpose, especially for small samples.

# KS test
ks.test(dt,"pnorm", exact = T)
#> 
#>  Exact one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
#> 
#> data:  dt
#> D = 0.03272, p-value = 0.2296
#> alternative hypothesis: two-sided

Interpretation: The test statistic (D) is 0.017067, and the p-value is 0.9982. With a high p-value, there is no significant evidence to reject the null hypothesis, indicating that the data does not significantly differ from the assumed distribution in a two-sided test.

Q-Q plot

A Q-Q plot is a graphical tool to assess whether a dataset follows a particular distribution, such as the normal distribution. It plots the quantiles of the sample data against the quantiles of the theoretical distribution. If the points fall approximately along a straight line, the data is likely normally distributed. Deviations from the line (e.g., curves or outliers) suggest departures from normality.

# QQ plot
qqnorm(dt)
qqnorm(data$Age)

Interpretation: In the first chart, the points do not deviate significantly from the line, indicating that the data, dt, is normally distributed. However, in the second chart, the points deviate from the line, forming an S-shaped curve, which suggests that the data is not normally distributed.

Histogram

A histogram is a visual representation of the distribution of a dataset. It divides the data into bins and displays the frequency of observations in each bin.For normality testing, the shape of the histogram is compared to the bell-shaped curve of a normal distribution. Symmetry, peak at the mean, and tapering tails are indicators of normality. While useful for a quick visual check, histograms can be subjective and less reliable for small sample sizes.

# Histogram
sequence <- seq(min(dt), max(dt), 0.2)
fun <- dnorm(sequence, mean = mean(dt), sd = sd(dt))
hist(dt,
     main = "data",
     ylab = "Frequency",
     xlab="dt",
     col="skyblue", probability = T)
lines(sequence, fun, col="blue", lwd=2)

Interpretation: Since, the chart seems to be symmetric with peak at mean and tapering tails which indicates that the data is normally distributed.
