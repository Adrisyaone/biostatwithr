Measures of Central Tendency

Introduction

A measure of central tendency is a single value that attempts to describe a data set by identifying the “center” or “typical” value of the distribution. The three classical measures are the mean, median, and mode; we also cover quartiles, percentiles, and deciles, which are measures of position closely related to the median.

Throughout this section we will use three running data sets so every formula can be demonstrated with a full, step-by-step manual calculation:

  • Dataset A (raw/ungrouped data): Fasting blood glucose (mg/dL) of 11 patients attending a diabetes clinic:

    88, 92, 95, 98, 101, 104, 108, 112, 118, 125, 250

  • Dataset B (frequency table / discrete data): Number of children in 40 households in a village survey.

    Number of children (\(x\)) Frequency (\(f\))
    0 6
    1 10
    2 14
    3 7
    4 3
    Total 40

  • Data Set C (grouped frequency distribution): Age (in years) of 50 patients admitted with hypertension, grouped into class intervals.

    Class interval Frequency (\(f\))
    30–39 4
    40–49 9
    50–59 15
    60–69 13
    70–79 7
    80–89 2
    Total 50

The Arithmetic Mean

Definition

The arithmetic mean (commonly just called “the mean” or “average”) is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations.

Formula

For a population of size \(N\): \[\mu = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i}{N}\]

For a sample of size \(n\): \[\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n}\]

Manual Calculation

A. Raw (Ungrouped) Data

Using Data Set A (fasting glucose, mg/dL): 88, 92, 95, 98, 101, 104, 108, 112, 118, 125, 250 (\(n = 11\))

Step 1. Sum all observations: \[\sum x_i = 88+92+95+98+101+104+108+112+118+125+250 = 1291\]

Step 2. Divide by \(n\): \[\bar{x} = \frac{1291}{11} = 117.4 \text{ mg/dL}\]

Notice that the single extreme value (250 mg/dL, representing an undiagnosed diabetic outlier) pulls the mean upward — this illustrates the mean’s sensitivity to outliers, discussed further below.

B. Frequency Table (Ungrouped, Discrete Data)

Data Set B — number of children (\(x\)) per household, surveyed in 40 households:

Number of children (\(x\)) Frequency (\(f\)) \(f \times x\)
0 6 0
1 10 10
2 14 28
3 7 21
4 3 12
Total \(n = \sum f = 40\) \(\sum fx = 71\)

Formula: \(\bar{x} = \dfrac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}\)

Step 1. Compute \(f \times x\) for each row (shown above).

Step 2. Sum the \(fx\) column: \(\sum fx = 0+10+28+21+12 = 71\).

Step 3. Sum the frequency column: \(\sum f = 6+10+14+7+3 = 40\).

Step 4. Divide: \[\bar{x} = \frac{71}{40} = 1.775 \approx 1.8 \text{ children per household}\]

C. Grouped Frequency Distribution

Data Set C is a age (in years) of 50 hypertension patients:

Class interval Midpoint (\(m\)) Frequency (\(f\)) \(f \times m\)
30–39 34.5 4 138.0
40–49 44.5 9 400.5
50–59 54.5 15 817.5
60–69 64.5 13 838.5
70–79 74.5 7 521.5
80–89 84.5 2 169.0
Total - \(n = 50\) \(\sum fm = 2885.0\)

Formula: \(\bar{x} = \dfrac{\sum f_i m_i}{\sum f_i}\), where \(m_i\) is the midpoint of each class.

Step 1. Find the midpoint of each class: \(m = \dfrac{\text{lower limit} + \text{upper limit}}{2}\). For 30–39: \(m = (30+39)/2 = 34.5\) (using continuous class boundaries 29.5–39.5 is also acceptable and gives the same midpoint here). Step 2. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency (\(f \times m\)), shown above. Step 3. Sum the \(fm\) column: \(2885.0\). Step 4. Divide by total frequency: \[\bar{x} = \frac{2885.0}{50} = 57.7 \text{ years}\]

The grouped mean is an approximation because individual raw values inside each class are unknown; we assume every observation in a class is located at the midpoint. This assumption introduces a small amount of grouping error.

Interpretation

The arithmetic mean represents the “balance point” of the data. If the data were weights on a see-saw, the mean is where it would balance.

Advantages

  • Uses every observation in the data set.
  • Algebraically tractable (has well-defined mathematical properties, e.g., \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0\)), making it the basis for further statistics (variance, regression, etc.).
  • Familiar and easy to interpret.
  • Stable from sample to sample (least sampling variability among the three central tendency measures, under normality).

Disadvantages

  • Highly sensitive to outliers and skewed data (as shown above, one value of 250 pulled the mean far above where most data points lie).
  • Cannot be computed for open-ended class intervals or for nominal/ordinal data.
  • Can produce a value that does not correspond to any actual observation (e.g., “1.8 children”), which may seem unnatural.

Appropriate Use

The mean is most appropriate when data are symmetric (approximately normally distributed) and free of major outliers, and when the variable is measured on an interval or ratio scale.

Public Health Example

The mean is used to report the average birth weight of newborns in a hospital, the mean systolic blood pressure in a screened population, or the mean length of hospital stay for a given diagnosis.

Weighted Mean

Definition

The weighted mean is a generalization of the arithmetic mean in which each observation is multiplied by a weight reflecting its relative importance, rather than being treated equally.

Formula

\[\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i}\]

where \(w_i\) is the weight assigned to value \(x_i\).

Worked Example (Public Health)

A district’s overall infant mortality rate (IMR) is calculated by combining IMRs from three sub-districts, weighted by the number of live births in each (not by simply averaging the three rates):

Sub-district IMR per 1000 live births (\(x\)) Live births (weight \(w\)) \(w \times x\)
A 25 2000 50,000
B 40 500 20,000
C 18 3500 63,000
Total \(\sum w = 6000\) \(\sum wx = 133{,}000\)

Step 1. Multiply each rate by its weight (live births): shown above. Step 2. Sum the weighted values: \(133{,}000\). Step 3. Sum the weights: \(6000\). Step 4. Divide: \[\bar{x}_w = \frac{133{,}000}{6000} = 22.17 \text{ per } 1000 \text{ live births}\]

Notice that the naive (unweighted) average of 25, 40, and 18 would be \(27.7\) — quite different, and misleading, because it ignores that sub-district B has far fewer births and should count less toward the district total.

Interpretation

The weighted mean reflects the true overall rate/value when subgroups contribute unequally to the total.

Advantages

  • Accounts for differing sample sizes or importance across subgroups.
  • Prevents small subgroups from distorting the overall estimate.

Disadvantages

  • Requires accurate and appropriate weights; incorrect weights produce a misleading result.
  • More complex to compute and explain than the simple mean.

Appropriate Use

Used whenever combining rates or averages from groups of unequal size. Standardized mortality/morbidity rates, meta-analysis of study results (weighted by sample size or inverse variance), grade point averages (weighted by credit hours).

Public Health Example

Age-standardized mortality rates, which combine age-specific death rates using a standard population’s age distribution as weights, so that mortality rates can be fairly compared across populations with different age structures.

Geometric Mean

Definition

The geometric mean (GM) is the \(n\)-th root of the product of \(n\) observations. It is used for data that grow multiplicatively (e.g., rates of change, ratios, or log-normally distributed data such as antibody titers).

Formula

\[GM = \sqrt[n]{x_1 \times x_2 \times \cdots \times x_n} = \left( \prod_{i=1}^n x_i \right)^{1/n}\]

Equivalently, using logarithms (easier for manual calculation with many observations): \[GM = \exp\left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln(x_i)}{n} \right)\]

Manual Calculation

Suppose antibody titers (reciprocal dilution) from 4 patients after vaccination are: 8, 16, 32, 128.

Step 1. Multiply all values: \(8 \times 16 \times 32 \times 128 = 524{,}288\). Step 2. Take the \(n\)-th root (\(n = 4\)): \[GM = \sqrt[4]{524{,}288} = 26.9\]

Verification using logarithms: \(\ln 8 = 2.079\), \(\ln 16 = 2.773\), \(\ln 32 = 3.466\), \(\ln 128 = 4.852\). Sum \(= 13.17\). Mean of logs \(= 13.17/4 = 3.29\). \(GM = e^{3.29} = 26.9\) ✓.

Compare this to the arithmetic mean of the same data: \(\bar{x} = (8+16+32+128)/4 = 46\). The geometric mean (26.9) is always \(\le\) the arithmetic mean (46) for positive data, and is much less influenced by the largest value (128).

Interpretation

The geometric mean represents the central tendency of multiplicative or exponential processes; it is the appropriate “average” when data are ratios, growth rates, or span several orders of magnitude.

Advantages

  • Less affected by extreme high values than the arithmetic mean (dampens the effect of outliers because it operates on a log scale).
  • Appropriate for log-normally distributed data (very common in laboratory/biological measurements such as titers, viral loads, and PCR cycle thresholds).
  • Useful for averaging ratios, rates of growth, or index numbers.

Disadvantages

  • Undefined if any observation is zero or negative.
  • More difficult to compute manually and less intuitive to interpret than the arithmetic mean.
  • Not appropriate for additive data.

Appropriate Use

Used for serial dilution titers, viral load and antibody data, growth rates of populations or bacterial cultures, and any positively skewed, log-normal biological data.

Public Health Example

Reporting the geometric mean antibody titer post-vaccination in a clinical trial, or the geometric mean viral load in an HIV cohort, standard practice in immunology and virology because titers are log-normally distributed.

Harmonic Mean

Definition

The harmonic mean (HM) is the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals of the observations. It is most appropriate for averaging rates (e.g., speeds, rates per unit time) when the numerator is fixed.

Formula

\[HM = \frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{x_i}}\]

Manual Calculation

Suppose three health workers screen patients at rates of 20, 30, and 60 patients per hour, and each worked for the same total number of patients screened (not the same amount of time). Find the average screening rate.

Step 1. Take the reciprocal of each rate: \(1/20 = 0.0500\), \(1/30 = 0.0333\), \(1/60 = 0.0167\).

Step 2. Sum the reciprocals: \(0.0500+0.0333+0.0167 = 0.1000\).

Step 3. Divide \(n\) by this sum: \[HM = \frac{3}{0.1000} = 30 \text{ patients/hour}\]

Note that the arithmetic mean of the same three rates would be \((20+30+60)/3 = 36.7\), which would overstate the true average rate when the underlying quantity (patients screened) is fixed, not the time.

Interpretation

The harmonic mean gives the correct “average rate” when combining rates that share a common numerator (e.g., a fixed distance, fixed number of patients, or fixed dose).

Advantages

  • Correct choice for averaging rates and ratios where the denominator varies.
  • Strongly dampens the influence of large values — most conservative of the three Pythagorean means (\(HM \le GM \le AM\)).

Disadvantages

  • Undefined if any value is zero.
  • Rarely used and often misunderstood; easy to apply incorrectly.
  • Heavily influenced by small values.

Appropriate Use

Used when averaging rates such as speed, dose per kilogram, or performance rates, particularly when the events being counted (not the time) are held constant.

Public Health Example

Calculating the average turnaround time rate for laboratory tests processed at different rates by different technicians, or combining rates in person-time incidence calculations.

Relationship Among the Three Means

For any set of positive numbers that are not all identical: \[HM \le GM \le AM\]

The three means are equal only when all observations are identical.

The Median

Definition

The median is the middle value of a data set when the observations are arranged in ascending (or descending) order. It divides the data into two equal halves: 50% of observations lie at or below the median, and 50% lie at or above it.

Formula

For raw/ungrouped data with \(n\) observations sorted in order:

  • If \(n\) is odd: Median \(= x_{\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}\)
  • If \(n\) is even: Median \(= \dfrac{x_{(n/2)} + x_{(n/2+1)}}{2}\)

For grouped data: \[\text{Median} = L + \left( \frac{\frac{n}{2} - CF}{f} \right) \times h\]

where \(L\) = lower boundary of the median class, \(n\) = total frequency, \(CF\) = cumulative frequency before the median class, \(f\) = frequency of the median class, \(h\) = class width.

Manual Calculation

A. Raw Data

Dataset A sorted: 88, 92, 95, 98, 101, 104, 108, 112, 118, 125, 250 (\(n = 11\), odd)

Step 1. Confirm data are sorted (already sorted above).

Step 2. Since \(n = 11\) is odd, the median is the \(\left(\frac{11+1}{2}\right) = 6^{th}\) value.

Step 3. Count to the 6th value: 88(1), 92(2), 95(3), 98(4), 101(5), 104(6). \[\text{Median} = 104 \text{ mg/dL}\]

Notice the median (104) is much closer to where most of the data lie than the mean (117.4). Here, the median is not pulled by the outlier of 250.

B. Frequency Table

Data Set B (number of children per household):

\(x\) \(f\) Cumulative frequency (\(CF\))
0 6 6
1 10 16
2 14 30
3 7 37
4 3 40

Step 1. Build the cumulative frequency column (shown above).

Step 2. With \(n = 40\) (even), the median position is the average of the \(20^{th}\) and \(21^{st}\) values.

Step 3. Locate these positions using \(CF\): the \(17^{th}\)–\(30^{th}\) values all equal 2 (since \(CF\) jumps from 16 to 30 at \(x=2\)). Both the \(20^{th}\) and \(21^{st}\) values fall in this range.

Step 4. Calculation \[\text{Median} = \frac{2+2}{2} = 2 \text{ children}\]

C. Grouped Frequency Distribution

Data Set C (age of 50 hypertension patients):

Class \(f\) \(CF\)
30–39 4 4
40–49 9 13
50–59 15 28
60–69 13 41
70–79 7 48
80–89 2 50

Step 1. \(n/2 = 50/2 = 25\).

Step 2. Find the median class: the class where the cumulative frequency first reaches/exceeds 25. That is the 50–59 class (\(CF\) jumps from 13 to 28).

Step 3. Identify: \(L = 49.5\) (lower boundary), \(CF = 13\) (cumulative frequency before median class), \(f = 15\) (frequency of median class), \(h = 10\) (class width).

Step 4. Apply the formula: \[\text{Median} = 49.5 + \left( \frac{25 - 13}{15} \right) \times 10 = 49.5 + \left(\frac{12}{15}\right)\times 10 = 49.5 + 8.0 = 57.5 \text{ years}\]

Interpretation

The median is the value at the 50th percentile — half the observations fall below it, half above.

Advantages

  • Robust to outliers and skewed distributions (unaffected by extreme values, as shown above).
  • Can be computed for ordinal data (does not require interval/ratio scale).
  • Not distorted by open-ended class intervals.

Disadvantages

  • Does not use the actual value of every observation (only their rank/position), so it “wastes” information present in the data.
  • Less amenable to further algebraic/statistical manipulation than the mean.
  • Can be less stable (more sampling variability) than the mean for perfectly normal data.

Appropriate Use

Preferred over the mean for skewed distributions (e.g., income, hospital charges, length of stay) or when outliers are present, and for ordinal data.

Public Health Example

Median household income, median survival time in a cancer study (widely reported instead of mean survival because survival time is typically right-skewed and subject to censoring), median age at diagnosis.

The Mode

Definition

The mode is the value (or category) that occurs most frequently in a data set. Unlike the mean and median, the mode can be identified for nominal data, and a data set can have zero, one (unimodal), two (bimodal), or more (multimodal) modes.

Formula (Grouped Data)

\[\text{Mode} = L + \left( \frac{f_1 - f_0}{2f_1 - f_0 - f_2} \right) \times h\]

where \(L\) = lower boundary of the modal class, \(f_1\) = frequency of modal class, \(f_0\) = frequency of class before modal class, \(f_2\) = frequency of class after modal class, \(h\) = class width.

Manual Calculation

A. Raw Data

Data Set A: 88, 92, 95, 98, 101, 104, 108, 112, 118, 125, 250. Every value occurs exactly once, So, there is no mode for this data set.

Consider instead a small illustrative data set of patient temperatures (°F): 98.6, 99.1, 98.6, 100.2, 98.6, 99.1.

Step 1. Tally the frequency of each distinct value:

Value Frequency
98.6 3
99.1 2
100.2 1

Step 2. The value with the highest frequency is the mode:

\(Mode = 98.6°F\)

B. Frequency Table

For dataset B, the highest frequency is \(f = 14\), occurring at \(x = 2\). \[\text{Mode} = 2 \text{ children}\]

This simply corresponds to the category with the largest tally in the frequency table.

C. Grouped Frequency Distribution

For the dataset C, mode can be estimated using following steps:

Step 1. Identify the modal class - the class with the highest frequency: 50–59 (\(f_1 = 15\)).

Step 2. Identify neighboring frequencies: \(f_0 = 9\) (40–49 class, before), \(f_2 = 13\) (60–69 class, after).

Step 3. \(L = 49.5\), \(h = 10\).

Step 4. Apply the formula: \[\text{Mode} = 49.5 + \left( \frac{15-9}{2(15)-9-13} \right)\times 10 = 49.5 + \left(\frac{6}{8}\right)\times 10 = 49.5+7.5 = 57.0 \text{ years}\]

Interpretation

The mode identifies the most “popular” or common value/category — useful for describing the peak of a distribution or the most frequent category in nominal data.

Advantages

  • Only measure of central tendency valid for nominal data.
  • Not affected by extreme outliers.
  • Easy to understand intuitively; represents an actual, commonly-occurring value.
  • Directly identifies the most frequent category, useful for resource planning (e.g., “most common blood type needed”).

Disadvantages

  • May not exist, or may not be unique (bimodal/multimodal data).
  • Ignores much of the data (based only on the frequency of the most common value(s)).
  • Can be unstable in small samples — a small change in the data can shift the mode considerably.

Appropriate Use

Best used for categorical (nominal) data, or to describe the peak/most common value of any distribution alongside other measures.

Public Health Example

The modal blood type in a blood bank’s donor registry (to guide blood product stocking), or the modal age group affected in an outbreak (identifying which age group to target with an intervention).

Quartiles, Percentiles, and Deciles

Quartiles, percentiles, and deciles are collectively called measures of position (or quantiles) — they divide an ordered data set into equal-sized parts and are closely related to the median (which is both the 2nd quartile, the 50th percentile, and the 5th decile).

Definitions

  • Quartiles (\(Q_1, Q_2, Q_3\)): Divide data into 4 equal parts. \(Q_1\) = 25th percentile, \(Q_2\) = median (50th percentile), \(Q_3\) = 75th percentile.
  • Deciles (\(D_1 \ldots D_9\)): Divide data into 10 equal parts. \(D_5\) = median.
  • Percentiles (\(P_1 \ldots P_{99}\)): Divide data into 100 equal parts. \(P_{50}\) = median.

Formula (Grouped Data)

\[Q_k = L + \left( \frac{\frac{kn}{4} - CF}{f} \right)\times h \quad (k=1,2,3)\] \[P_k = L + \left( \frac{\frac{kn}{100} - CF}{f} \right)\times h \quad (k=1,\ldots,99)\] \[D_k = L + \left( \frac{\frac{kn}{10} - CF}{f} \right)\times h \quad (k=1,\ldots,9)\]

These all use the same logic as the median formula, simply replacing \(n/2\) with the appropriate fraction of \(n\).

Manual Calculation — A. Raw Data

Using Data Set A (sorted): 88, 92, 95, 98, 101, 104, 108, 112, 118, 125, 250 (\(n=11\))

Finding \(Q_1\) (25th percentile):

Step 1. Compute the position: \(\dfrac{n+1}{4} = \dfrac{12}{4} = 3^{rd}\) position. Step 2. The 3rd value in the ordered list is 95. \[Q_1 = 95 \text{ mg/dL}\]

Finding \(Q_3\) (75th percentile):

Step 1. Compute the position: \(\dfrac{3(n+1)}{4} = \dfrac{3 \times 12}{4} = 9^{th}\) position. Step 2. The 9th value is 118. \[Q_3 = 118 \text{ mg/dL}\]

Interquartile Range (a byproduct, used in Section 3): \(IQR = Q_3 - Q_1 = 118 - 95 = 23\).

Several valid methods exist for computing quantile positions with raw data (e.g., the method shown above vs. linear interpolation methods used by software like R, Excel, or SPSS). Minor differences between hand calculations and software output are normal and expected; always report which method/software was used.

Manual Calculation — C. Grouped Frequency Distribution

Using Data Set C (age of 50 hypertension patients), find \(Q_1\) and \(P_{90}\).

Finding \(Q_1\):

Step 1. Position: \(\dfrac{kn}{4} = \dfrac{1 \times 50}{4} = 12.5\). Step 2. Find the class where cumulative frequency first reaches 12.5: looking at the \(CF\) column (4, 13, 28, 41, 48, 50), this occurs in the 40–49 class (\(CF\) jumps from 4 to 13). Step 3. \(L=39.5\), \(CF_{\text{before}}=4\), \(f=9\), \(h=10\). Step 4. \[Q_1 = 39.5 + \left(\frac{12.5-4}{9}\right)\times 10 = 39.5+9.44 = 48.9 \text{ years}\]

Finding \(P_{90}\) (90th percentile):

Step 1. Position: \(\dfrac{kn}{100} = \dfrac{90\times 50}{100}=45\). Step 2. Find the class where \(CF\) first reaches 45: the 70–79 class (\(CF\) jumps from 41 to 48). Step 3. \(L=69.5\), \(CF_{\text{before}}=41\), \(f=7\), \(h=10\). Step 4. \[P_{90} = 69.5 + \left(\frac{45-41}{7}\right)\times 10 = 69.5+5.71 = 75.2 \text{ years}\]

Interpretation

\(Q_1 = 48.9\) years means 25% of hypertension patients in this sample are younger than 48.9 years. \(P_{90} = 75.2\) years means 90% of patients are younger than 75.2 years (only 10% are older).

Advantages

  • Provide detailed information about the shape and spread of a distribution beyond a single center value.
  • Robust to outliers (like the median).
  • Useful for constructing growth charts, reference ranges, and box plots.

Disadvantages

  • Do not summarize the data in a single number; require reporting multiple values.
  • Can be sensitive to the choice of interpolation method, especially in small samples.

Appropriate Use

Used whenever the position of an individual within a distribution matters — growth charts (a child’s weight at the 15th percentile), setting clinical reference ranges, identifying high-risk groups (e.g., the top decile of BMI).

Public Health Example

Pediatric growth charts (WHO/CDC) report a child’s weight-for-age and height-for-age as a percentile, e.g., “this infant’s weight is at the 25th percentile,” meaning 25% of same-age, same-sex infants weigh less.

Comparing All Measures of Central Tendency

Measure Data type Uses all data? Affected by outliers? Unique? Algebraically tractable?
Arithmetic mean Interval/ratio Yes Highly Yes Yes
Weighted mean Interval/ratio Yes Highly Yes Yes
Geometric mean Ratio (positive only) Yes Moderately Yes Yes (on log scale)
Harmonic mean Ratio (positive only) Yes Least (of the 3 means) Yes Yes
Median Ordinal, interval, ratio No (only rank used) No (robust) Yes Limited
Mode Nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio No No (robust) Not always No
Quartiles/Percentiles/Deciles Ordinal, interval, ratio No (rank-based) No (robust) Yes Limited

Choosing the Right Measure — Effect of Skewness

flowchart TD
    A[Is the variable numeric?] -->|No, categorical| B[Use Mode]
    A -->|Yes| C{Is the distribution roughly symmetric, no major outliers?}
    C -->|Yes| D[Use Mean]
    C -->|No: skewed or has outliers| E[Use Median]
    D --> F{Is the data multiplicative / ratios / log-normal, e.g. titers?}
    F -->|Yes| G[Consider Geometric Mean]
    F -->|No| D

Relationship between mean, median, and skewness:

  • Symmetric distribution: Mean ≈ Median ≈ Mode.
  • Right-skewed (positively skewed) distribution (e.g., income, hospital charges, our Data Set A with the outlier of 250): Mean > Median > Mode.
  • Left-skewed (negatively skewed) distribution (e.g., age at death from a disease mostly affecting the elderly): Mean < Median < Mode.

In Data Set A: Mean = 117.4, Median = 104 — the mean exceeds the median, correctly signaling a right-skewed distribution due to the outlier.

Summary

  • The mean is the most commonly used and mathematically useful measure, but it is sensitive to outliers and skewness.
  • The weighted mean correctly combines subgroup values with different levels of importance/sample size.
  • The geometric mean and harmonic mean are specialized means for multiplicative data and rates, respectively; \(HM \le GM \le AM\).
  • The median is robust to outliers and skewness and is preferred for skewed public health data such as income or survival time.
  • The mode is the only central tendency measure usable with nominal data.
  • Quartiles, percentiles, and deciles describe an individual’s position within a distribution and underlie growth charts and reference ranges.

Section 3 builds directly on these concepts by covering measures of dispersion, which quantify how spread out the data are around these central values.
