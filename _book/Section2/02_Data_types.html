Types of data and scales of measurement

Types of Data

Data collected in biostatistics and epidemiology generally fall into two broad categories: qualitative (categorical) and quantitative (numerical) data.

flowchart TD
    A[Types of Data] --> B[Qualitative / Categorical]
    A --> C[Quantitative / Numerical]
    B --> B1[Nominal]
    B --> B2[Ordinal]
    C --> C1[Discrete]
    C --> C2[Continuous]

Qualitative (Categorical) Data

Categorical data represent characteristics or attributes that place individuals into groups or categories. They cannot be meaningfully added, subtracted, or averaged.

  • Nominal data: Categories with no inherent order. Example: blood type (A, B, AB, O), sex, marital status, country of residence, vaccination status (vaccinated/unvaccinated).
  • Ordinal data: Categories with a meaningful order, but the intervals between categories are not necessarily equal. Example: cancer stage (I, II, III, IV), pain severity (mild, moderate, severe), socioeconomic status (low, middle, high), Likert scale responses (strongly disagree to strongly agree).

Quantitative (Numerical) Data

Numerical data represent measurable quantities and can be manipulated arithmetically.

  • Discrete data: Data that can take only specific, countable values (usually whole numbers), typically arising from counting. Example: number of children per household, number of hospital admissions per day, number of new COVID-19 cases per week, parity (number of live births).
  • Continuous data: Data that can take any value within a range, typically arising from measurement. Example: height, weight, blood pressure, serum cholesterol level, BMI, gestational age.

A useful rule of thumb: if you can meaningfully ask “how many?” the data are usually discrete; if you can ask “how much?” and fractional values make sense, the data are usually continuous.

Variables

A variable is any characteristic, number, or quantity that can be measured or counted and that can differ from one individual (or observation) to another.

Classification of Variables

flowchart TD
    V[Variables] --> Q[Quantitative]
    V --> C[Qualitative]
    Q --> QD[Discrete]
    Q --> QC[Continuous]
    C --> CN[Nominal]
    C --> CO[Ordinal]
    V --> R[By Role in Analysis]
    R --> R1[Independent / Exposure Variable]
    R --> R2[Dependent / Outcome Variable]
    R --> R3[Confounding Variable]

  • Independent variable (exposure/predictor): The variable believed to influence or predict the outcome. Example: smoking status.
  • Dependent variable (outcome/response): The variable being predicted or explained. Example: lung cancer status.
  • Confounding variable: A variable associated with both the exposure and the outcome that can distort the observed association if not controlled for. Example: age as a confounder in the relationship between physical activity and heart disease.

Public health example: In a study of the relationship between air pollution (independent variable measured as PM2.5 concentration, a continuous variable) and asthma attacks (dependent variable, a discrete count), age and smoking status might act as confounding variables.

Scales of Measurement

Stanley Smith Stevens (1946) classified measurement into four scales(Stevens 1946). Understanding the scale of measurement of a variable is essential because it determines which descriptive statistics and which statistical tests are appropriate.

Scale Order? Equal intervals? True zero? Allowed operations Examples
Nominal No No No Counting, mode, frequencies Blood type, sex, ethnicity
Ordinal Yes No No Median, percentiles, ranking Cancer stage, pain scale
Interval Yes Yes No (arbitrary zero) Mean, SD, addition/subtraction Temperature (°C), IQ score
Ratio Yes Yes Yes (true zero) All arithmetic, including ratios Weight, height, age, blood pressure

A simple test for ratio scale: does “zero” mean “none of the quantity,” and does it make sense to say “20 kg is twice as heavy as 10 kg”? If yes, it is a ratio scale. Temperature in Celsius fails this test (0°C is not “no temperature,” and 20°C is not “twice as hot” as 10°C), so it is only an interval scale.

Nominal and ordinal scales are collectively referred to as categorical, while interval and ratio scales are collectively referred to as numerical/quantitative. Most public health and clinical measurements (weight, blood pressure, cell counts, survival time) are on the ratio scale.

Summary

  • Data are broadly classified as qualitative (nominal, ordinal) or quantitative (discrete, continuous).

  • Variables may serve as independent, dependent, or confounding variables in an analysis.

  • The scale of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) determines which statistical summaries and tests are valid.

References

Stevens, S. S. 1946. “On the Theory of Scales of Measurement.” Science 103 (2684): 677–80. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.103.2684.677.
