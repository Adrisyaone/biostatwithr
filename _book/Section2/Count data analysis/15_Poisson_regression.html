Count Data analysis

Count data

The data consisting of non-negative integers values which is the count of occurrence of events. Due to the discrete nature, the count data can’t be treated as continuous variables. The categorical analysis methods are also not appropriate. The examples of count data are:

a) Number of risk factors of NCDs present in a person

b) Number of people visiting doctor’s office per month

Poisson Distribution

It is the distribution if we want to calculate the probabilities of the number of ocurences of some phenomenon in some space or time interval, often measured as the rate (average given the measired space).

\[ f(x)= \frac{e^{-\lambda}*\lambda^x}{x!}, x=1,2,... \]

\[ E(x)=Var(x) = lambda \]

\(\lambda\) (rate) is the average number of some success in sometime span.

Poisson Regression

Poisson regression is particularly useful for:

  • Modeling the count of events occurring within a fixed period or space.
  • Predicting counts based on predictor variables.

The general form of the Poisson regression model is:

\[ \log(\lambda_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \ldots + \beta_n X_n \]

Assumptions of Poisson Regression

  1. Poisson Distribution: The response variable ( Y_i ) follows a Poisson distribution. \[ Y_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_i) \]

  2. Log Link Function: The logarithm of the mean ( _i ) is linearly related to the predictors. \[ \log(\lambda_i) = X\beta \]

  3. Equality of Mean and Variance: The mean and variance of the response variable are equal. \[ E(Y_i) = Var(Y_i) = \lambda_i \]

  4. Independence: Observations are independent of each other.

  5. No Multi-collinearity: Predictor variables should not exhibit high collinearity.

Interpreting coefficients as Incidence Rate Ratios (IRRs)

Because the log link is used, exponentiating the coefficients converts them from the log scale to incidence rate ratios — the multiplicative change in the expected count per one-unit increase in the predictor.

exp(cbind(IRR = coef(m_pois), confint(m_pois)))
#>                   IRR     2.5 %    97.5 %
#> (Intercept) 0.3679788 0.2939149 0.4572799
#> age_c       1.8992313 1.8010611 2.0059258
#> smoker      1.1063528 0.9563549 1.2783456
#> chronic     1.5523778 1.4596413 1.6507482
#> insured     1.3493914 1.1392668 1.6069800

For example, an IRR of 1.68 for chronic means each additional chronic condition is associated with a 68% increase in expected ED visits, holding other predictors constant.

Modeling rates with an offset

When counts are observed over varying amounts of time or exposure (e.g., person-years of follow-up), an offset is used so the model estimates a rate rather than a raw count. If dat$time held each patient’s follow-up time, the model would be:

m_rate <- glm(ed_visits ~ age_c + smoker + chronic + insured,
              family = poisson(link = "log"),
              offset = log(time), data = dat)

The offset enters the model with a fixed coefficient of 1, so log(lambda_i / time_i) is modeled instead of log(lambda_i) — the fitted coefficients are then interpreted as rate ratios per unit time.

Goodness of fit

1 - pchisq(deviance(m_pois), df = m_pois$df.residual)
#> [1] 0

A small p-value here suggests the model does not fit well — this is the first diagnostic that motivates checking for overdispersion and excess zeros, covered next.

Overdispersion and Zero-Inflated Count Models

Why Poisson Regression Is Often Not Enough

Poisson regression, which rests on a strict assumption: the mean and the variance of the count are equal, \(E(Y_i)=Var(Y_i)=\lambda_i\). Real health-count data almost never behave this politely. Two problems show up constantly in clinical and public-health data:

  1. Overdispersion — the observed variance is larger than the mean (sometimes far larger), because of unmeasured heterogeneity between patients, clustering of events within a person, or contagion-type processes (one hospitalization increases the risk of another).
  2. Excess zeros — the data contain more zero counts than a Poisson (or even a Negative Binomial) distribution would predict, because some individuals are structurally incapable of generating a positive count (e.g., a never-smoker cannot have a smoking-related complication count above zero) while others simply happened to have a zero during the observation window.

Ignoring either problem does not just produce a “slightly wrong” model — it produces standard errors that are too small, which inflates Type-I error and makes non-significant predictors look significant. This chapter builds, step by step, the toolbox used to diagnose and correct these problems:

  • Negative Binomial (NB) regression — corrects overdispersion.
  • Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP) regression — corrects excess zeros when counts are otherwise equidispersed.
  • Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) regression — corrects excess zeros and overdispersion simultaneously.
  • A short note on Hurdle models, a close relative of the zero-inflated family.

Every model below is fit on the same worked health example so the numbers can be compared directly.

Worked Example: Emergency Department (ED) Visits

Imagine a cohort of 400 adult patients followed for one year, and we count each patient’s number of Emergency Department visits. Predictors collected at baseline are:

a) age — age in years

b) smoker — current smoker (1) vs. non-smoker (0)

c) chronic — number of chronic conditions (0–4): diabetes, hypertension, COPD, heart disease

d) insured — has health insurance (1) vs. does not (0)

This is a realistic setting for two separate zero-generating mechanisms: some patients are simply not “at risk” of an ED visit in a given year (healthy, well-controlled, good access to primary care — a structural zero), while others are at risk but simply happened not to visit during the window (a sampling zero, drawn from a Poisson/NB process). This is exactly the situation zero-inflated models were built for.

The simulated dataset (ed_visits.csv, \(n=400\)) used throughout this chapter is saved alongside this file so every result below is fully reproducible.

Both signs point away from a plain Poisson model, but we fit it first anyway, because the diagnostics it produces are exactly what justify moving to the more complex models.

Interpretation

The dispersion statistic is about 4.1 (should be \(\approx 1\) under a true Poisson model), and dispersiontest() rejects equidispersion at \(p<0.001\). The model also badly under-predicts zeros: Poisson expects only about 41% zero visits, but 66% are observed in the data. Both diagnostics say the same thing: Poisson regression is not adequate here, and any p-values from m_pois should not be trusted — they are too optimistic (SEs too small). This is the standard trigger for moving to Negative Binomial regression.

Negative Binomial Regression

The model

The Negative Binomial (NB2, the standard parameterization) extends Poisson by adding a dispersion parameter \(\alpha>0\):

\[ Var(Y_i) = \mu_i + \alpha\,\mu_i^2 \]

When \(\alpha \to 0\), NB collapses back to Poisson (\(Var=\mu\)), which is why Poisson is a special case of NB and the two can be compared with a likelihood-ratio test. The mean structure is unchanged, \(\log(\mu_i)=X_i\beta\), so the interpretation of coefficients is identical to Poisson (incidence rate ratios, IRRs) — only the variance (and therefore the standard errors and p-values) is corrected.

When to use Negative Binomial instead of Poisson

  • The Pearson/deviance dispersion statistic is noticeably \(>1\) (commonly used rule of thumb: \(>1.5\)–\(2\) deserves attention; here it is \(4.1\)).
  • dispersiontest() (or an equivalent LR/score test) rejects equidispersion.
  • Overdispersion is plausibly due to unobserved heterogeneity between subjects rather than a distinct “always-zero” subpopulation (if the zeros look structurally different from the rest, consider zero-inflated models first — see below).
  • There is no strong a-priori reason to model the zeros with a separate process — NB simply lets the whole distribution be more spread out, including allowing more zeros than Poisson.

Interpretation

odTest() gives a large LR statistic with \(p<0.001\): overdispersion is real, and NB is a significantly better fit than Poisson (AIC drops from about 1654 to about 1144). The estimated dispersion parameter \(\hat\theta \approx 0.39\) (equivalently \(\hat\alpha = 1/\theta \approx 2.6\)) is far from the Poisson limit of \(\theta\to\infty\), confirming strong overdispersion.

Reading the IRRs: each additional chronic condition multiplies the expected number of ED visits by about \(e^{0.52}\approx1.68\) (68% more visits per chronic condition, holding other variables fixed), and each 10-year increase in age multiplies expected visits by about \(e^{0.66}\approx1.93\). Smoking status and insurance are no longer significant once overdispersion is properly accounted for (their Poisson p-values were artificially small).

The NB model also predicts the zero proportion much better than Poisson (62% vs. the 66% observed) — but not perfectly. That residual gap is the signature of excess zeros that NB alone cannot fully explain, which motivates the zero-inflated models next.

Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP) Regression

The model

ZIP treats the data as coming from a two-component mixture:

  • With probability \(\pi_i\), the observation is a “certain” (structural) zero — the patient is simply not in the at-risk population for this event.
  • With probability \(1-\pi_i\), the observation comes from a standard Poisson process with mean \(\mu_i\) (which can itself produce a zero by chance).

\[ P(Y_i = 0) = \pi_i + (1-\pi_i)e^{-\mu_i} \qquad\qquad P(Y_i = y) = (1-\pi_i)\,\frac{e^{-\mu_i}\mu_i^{y}}{y!},\ \ y=1,2,\dots \]

Two linear predictors are estimated simultaneously:

\[ \log(\mu_i) = X_i\beta \quad \text{(count model — "how many events, if any risk exists")} \]

\[ \text{logit}(\pi_i) = Z_i\gamma \quad \text{(zero-inflation model — "probability of being structurally not at risk")} \]

When to use ZIP

  • There is a substantively meaningful subgroup that cannot experience the event at all (e.g., patients with no disease exposure, no relevant risk factor, or excellent disease control), distinct from patients who are at risk but simply recorded a zero.
  • The proportion of zeros observed is clearly larger than a Poisson model predicts.
  • Among the “at-risk” patients, the counts themselves are close to equidispersed once the structural zeros are separated out (if they remain overdispersed even after removing structural zeros, ZINB is more appropriate — see the AIC comparison below).

R code

m_zip <- zeroinfl(ed_visits ~ age_c + smoker + chronic + insured |

Interpretation

Count component (IRR interpretation, same as Poisson/NB):

exp(coef(m_zip, "count"))
#> (Intercept)       age_c      smoker     chronic     insured 
#>    1.734249    1.313581    1.152634    1.289766    1.366942

Among patients who are “at risk,” each additional chronic condition is associated with about \(e^{0.25}\approx1.29\) times as many ED visits, and each 10 years of age with about \(e^{0.27}\approx1.31\) times as many visits.

Zero-inflation component (odds-ratio interpretation):

exp(coef(m_zip, "zero"))
#> (Intercept)       age_c      smoker     chronic 
#>   4.8567241   0.5043240   0.8704710   0.6519543

These coefficients describe the odds of being a structural (certain) zero. A negative coefficient means the predictor makes structural-zero membership less likely (i.e., pushes the patient toward the “at-risk” group). Here, older age and a higher chronic-disease count both significantly reduce the odds of being a “never visits the ED” patient (odds ratios around \(e^{-0.68}\approx0.51\) and \(e^{-0.40}\approx0.67\) per unit, respectively) — clinically sensible, since sicker and older patients are less likely to be entirely free of ED risk.

Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) Regression

The model

ZINB is the natural extension when both problems are present: excess zeros and overdispersion among the positive counts. It uses the same two-part mixture structure as ZIP, but replaces the Poisson count component with a Negative Binomial:

\[ P(Y_i=0)=\pi_i+(1-\pi_i)\left(\frac{\alpha^{-1}}{\alpha^{-1}+\mu_i}\right)^{\alpha^{-1}} \]

\[ P(Y_i=y)=(1-\pi_i)\,\frac{\Gamma(y+\alpha^{-1})}{y!\,\Gamma(\alpha^{-1})}\left(\frac{\alpha^{-1}}{\alpha^{-1}+\mu_i}\right)^{\alpha^{-1}}\left(\frac{\mu_i}{\alpha^{-1}+\mu_i}\right)^{y},\ \ y=1,2,\dots \]

When to use ZINB

  • Excess zeros and overdispersion are both present in the non-zero counts (the most common situation in real clinical utilization/morbidity data).
  • A plain NB model still under-predicts the zero count noticeably.
  • A plain ZIP model still shows overdispersion in its Pearson residuals, or has a worse AIC/BIC than NB despite fixing the zero problem (exactly what happens in this example, below).

Interpretation

Count component (IRR):

exp(coef(m_zinb, "count"))
#> (Intercept)       age_c      smoker     chronic     insured 
#>    1.588711    1.338261    1.069467    1.329073    1.338538

Zero-inflation component (OR of being a structural zero):

exp(coef(m_zinb, "zero"))
#> (Intercept)       age_c      smoker     chronic 
#>   4.0570830   0.5101870   0.8645866   0.6692264

The estimated dispersion parameter here (Log(theta) in the output, back-transformed) corresponds to \(\hat\theta\approx2.8\) (\(\hat\alpha\approx0.35\)) — much closer to the true amount of overdispersion in the at-risk population than the plain NB model’s inflated \(\hat\alpha\approx2.6\). This is an important lesson: when zero-inflation is ignored, a plain NB model tends to overestimate the dispersion parameter, because it is forced to use \(\alpha\) to absorb both genuine overdispersion and the excess-zero problem at once. Separating the two mechanisms (as ZINB does) recovers a more accurate picture of both.

The count-model IRRs and zero-inflation odds ratios are similar in direction and magnitude to the ZIP model, but the standard errors and the fit are more appropriate once the leftover overdispersion is modeled properly.

Notice the instructive (and common) pattern: fixing only one problem is not always enough. ZIP fixes the zero problem but still assumes an equidispersed Poisson for the count part, so it actually fits worse than plain NB in this example, because the remaining overdispersion among positive counts is left unmodeled. Formal comparison tests

Nested comparison (Poisson vs. NB) — already shown above with odTest().

Non-nested / boundary comparisons (Vuong test) — Poisson vs. ZIP, or NB vs. ZINB, are not compared cleanly with a standard likelihood-ratio test because the models are nested only at a parameter boundary (e.g., \(\pi_i \to 0\) for all \(i\)). The conventional tool is Vuong’s (1989) test, implemented in pscl::vuong():

vuong(m_pois, m_zip)

#> (test-statistic is asymptotically distributed N(0,1) under the
#>  null that the models are indistinguishible)

#> AIC-corrected         -7.566119 model2 > model1 1.9227e-14
#> BIC-corrected         -7.318688 model2 > model1 1.2520e-13
vuong(m_nb, m_zinb)
#> Vuong Non-Nested Hypothesis Test-Statistic: 
#> (test-statistic is asymptotically distributed N(0,1) under the
#>  null that the models are indistinguishible)
#> -------------------------------------------------------------
#>               Vuong z-statistic             H_A    p-value
#> Raw                   -4.673061 model2 > model1 1.4837e-06

#> BIC-corrected         -3.064501 model2 > model1  0.0010902

A significantly positive Vuong statistic favors the first model listed; a significantly negative statistic favors the second. In this example both tests favor the zero-inflated model over its non-inflated counterpart.

A Related Alternative: Hurdle Models

A hurdle model is a close cousin of the zero-inflated family, and is worth knowing about even though it is not fit in detail here. The key conceptual difference:

  • Zero-inflated models (ZIP/ZINB): zeros can come from two sources — structural zeros and sampling zeros from the count process (which can also itself produce a zero).
  • Hurdle models: all zeros come from a single binary “hurdle” process (did the event occur at all — yes/no); conditional on crossing the hurdle, the positive counts follow a zero-truncated Poisson or NB distribution. There is no separate “chance” zero from the count process — once the hurdle is crossed, the count is guaranteed to be \(\geq1\).

Use a hurdle model instead of a zero-inflated model when the two processes (“does the event happen at all” vs. “how many times, given it happens”) are conceptually cleaner than “structural vs. sampling zero,” or when you want a single unambiguous interpretation of the zero mechanism. In R: pscl::hurdle(y ~ x1 + x2 | x1 + x2, dist = "negbin"). Model selection between hurdle and zero-inflated forms again relies on AIC/BIC and substantive reasoning rather than a formal nested test, since the two are non-nested.

Decision Guide: Which Model Should I Use?

Start: you have a count outcome (e.g., number of ED visits, hospitalizations, symptom episodes).

Step 1 — Check dispersion. Is \(Var(Y)\) much larger than \(Mean(Y)\) (dispersion statistic \(\gg1\), dispersiontest()/odTest() significant)?

  • No (roughly equidispersed): go to Step 2a.
  • Yes (overdispersed): go to Step 2b.

Step 2a — No overdispersion. Are there more zeros than the Poisson model predicts?

  • No: use Poisson regression.
  • Yes: use Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP).

Step 2b — Overdispersion present. Are there still more zeros than the Negative Binomial model predicts?

  • No: use Negative Binomial (NB).
  • Yes: use Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) (or a Hurdle NB model if the “all zeros share one mechanism” framing fits better substantively).

Summary

Count outcomes in health research (ED visits, hospitalizations, falls, seizure counts, number of comorbid risk factors) very often violate the Poisson assumption of equal mean and variance, and frequently show more zeros than any single, simple distribution predicts. The practical workflow is:

  1. Fit Poisson first; always check the dispersion statistic and compare observed vs. predicted zeros.
  2. If only overdispersion is present, move to Negative Binomial.
  3. If only excess zeros are present (counts otherwise well-behaved), move to Zero-Inflated Poisson.
  4. If both problems are present — the most common situation in practice — move to Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial.
  5. Use AIC/BIC and the Vuong test to choose between non-nested alternatives, and odTest() for the nested Poisson-vs-NB comparison; always sanity-check the substantive story (is there really a distinct “not at risk” subgroup?) rather than choosing a model on fit statistics alone.
  6. Report incidence rate ratios (IRRs) from the count component and, for zero-inflated/hurdle models, odds ratios from the zero/hurdle component — these answer two different clinical questions (“how many events, given some risk” vs. “who is at risk at all”) and should be interpreted and reported separately.
