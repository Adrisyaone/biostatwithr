Comparing Two Independent Group Means

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. State the situation in which the means of two independent groups are compared, and translate a research question into \(H_0\)/\(H_1\).
  2. State the assumptions, formula, and correct interpretation of Student’s (pooled-variance) two-sample \(t\)-test.
  3. State when Welch’s \(t\)-test should be used instead, and explain how it differs from Student’s test.
  4. Calculate both test statistics by hand from raw data, including the pooled variance and Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.
  5. State the assumptions, formula, and correct interpretation of the Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test), the nonparametric alternative.
  6. Calculate the Mann-Whitney \(U\) statistic by hand, including handling of tied ranks.
  7. Reproduce all three procedures in R, using the WCGS data, and correctly choose among them based on formal checks of normality and variance homogeneity.

Comparing Two Independent Groups

A very common design in health research compares a numeric outcome between two separate, non-overlapping groups of subjects — for example, smokers vs. non-smokers, treatment vs. control, or (as in this chapter) Type A vs. Type B behaviour pattern. The word “independent” here means the two samples consist of different individuals with no natural pairing between them (contrast this with the paired designs covered in a later chapter).

We will use two grouping variables already present in the WCGS data:

  • dibpat — behaviour pattern, dichotomised as Type A (\(n=1{,}589\)) vs. Type B (\(n=1{,}565\)) — nearly equal group sizes, a good case to illustrate Student’s test.
  • chd69 — whether the subject had a coronary heart disease event by the end of follow-up, No (\(n=2{,}897\)) vs. Yes (\(n=257\)) — very unequal group sizes together with unequal variances and skew, a good case to illustrate Welch’s test and the Mann-Whitney U test.

Three procedures are commonly used:

Test Assumes equal variances? Assumes (approx.) normality? Uses
Student’s (pooled) \(t\)-test Yes Yes, in each group Raw values
Welch’s \(t\)-test No Yes, in each group Raw values

Student’s Two-Sample \(t\)-Test (Equal Variances)

Hypotheses

\[ H_0: \mu_1=\mu_2 \qquad H_1: \mu_1\ne\mu_2 \]

Assumptions

  1. The outcome is continuous numeric.
  2. The two samples are independent of each other, and observations within each sample are independent (a random sample from each population).
  3. Each population is (approximately) normally distributed, or each \(n\) is large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to apply.
  4. The two populations have equal variances (\(\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2\)) — often checked with an \(F\)-test (var.test()) or, preferably with non-normal data, Levene’s test.

Test statistic

The two sample variances are combined into a single pooled variance estimate:

\[ s_p^2=\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2} \tag{21.1}\]

\[ t=\frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{\sqrt{s_p^2\left(\dfrac1{n_1}+\dfrac1{n_2}\right)}} \qquad \sim\; t_{(n_1+n_2-2)} \text{ under } H_0 \tag{21.2}\]

Confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\)

\[ (\bar x_1-\bar x_2)\pm t_{(1-\alpha/2,\;n_1+n_2-2)}\sqrt{s_p^2\left(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}\right)} \]

The hand-calculated \(t=-1.847\), \(df=14\), \(p=0.086\) match R’s t.test(..., var.equal = TRUE) exactly. At the \(5\%\) level we do not reject \(H_0\) in this small subsample.

Look closely at the two group variances above: \(31.9\) vs. \(616.3\) — a nearly \(20\)-fold difference, purely a feature of this small, randomly drawn \(n=8\) subsample (the full-population variance ratio, shown later, is actually close to \(1\)). This is exactly why we should never eyeball a handful of numbers and assume equal variances — a formal test, on adequate sample sizes, is needed.

Welch’s \(t\)-Test (Unequal Variances)

When to use it

Use Welch’s test instead of Student’s when the assumption of equal population variances is doubtful — either because a formal test (an \(F\)-test or, more robustly, Levene’s test) rejects equal variances, or as a safe default: many statisticians now recommend using Welch’s test routinely, since it performs almost identically to Student’s test when variances are equal, but avoids the inflated Type I error that Student’s test can produce when variances differ, especially with unequal sample sizes.

Test statistic

Welch’s test does not pool the variances; each group contributes its own variance to the standard error:

\[ t=\frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}} \tag{23.1}\]

The degrees of freedom are not simply \(n_1+n_2-2\); instead the Welch-Satterthwaite approximation is used, which can take non-integer values:

\[ df_{Welch}=\frac{\left(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2} {\dfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\dfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}} \tag{23.2}\]

Manual (Hand) Calculation of Welch’s \(t\)-Test

Using the same two subsamples as above.

Step 1: Compute the (unpooled) standard error

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{31.93}{8}+\frac{616.27}{8}} \]

#> [1] 9.001364

Notice that because \(n_1=n_2=8\) here, \(SE_{Welch}=\sqrt{(s_1^2+s_2^2)/n}\) is numerically identical to \(SE_{pooled}=\sqrt{s_p^2 \times 2/n}\) from the Student’s test above — this is a general fact whenever the two sample sizes are equal. What differs between the two tests, in this equal-\(n\) case, is entirely the degrees of freedom.

Step 2: Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom

\[ df_{Welch}=\frac{(31.93/8+616.27/8)^2}{\dfrac{(31.93/8)^2}{7}+\dfrac{(616.27/8)^2}{7}} \]

#> [1] 7.723392

Because Group B’s variance so dominates Group A’s in this subsample, \(df_{Welch}=7.72\) — pulled down close to \(n_2-1=7\) (Welch’s df is always between \(\min(n_1,n_2)-1\) and \(n_1+n_2-2\); the more unequal the variances, the closer it moves to the smaller group’s own df).

Step 3: Test statistic and \(p\)-value

#>           t       df   p_value
#> 1 -1.846942 7.723392 0.1032826

Step 4: Verify against R

t.test(x1, x2, var.equal = FALSE)
#> 
#>  Welch Two Sample t-test
#> 
#> data:  x1 and x2
#> t = -1.8, df = 7.7, p-value = 0.1
#> alternative hypothesis: true difference in means is not equal to 0
#> 95 percent confidence interval:
#>  -37.512   4.262
#> sample estimates:
#> mean of x mean of y 
#>     166.8     183.4

The hand-calculated values match R’s default t.test() (Welch’s is R’s default for two-sample tests) exactly: \(t=-1.847\) (identical to Student’s, as expected with equal \(n\)), but \(df=7.72\) (much smaller than Student’s \(14\)), giving a wider, more conservative \(p\)-value (\(0.103\) vs. \(0.086\)) and a wider confidence interval. This illustrates precisely why Welch’s test matters even when the two \(t\)-statistics coincide: unequal variances erode the effective degrees of freedom, and ignoring that (as Student’s test does) can overstate precision.

Choosing between them: check variance homogeneity formally

var.test(weight ~ dibpat, data = wcgs)          # F-test (sensitive to non-normality)
#> 
#>  F test to compare two variances
#> 
#> data:  weight by dibpat
#> F = 1.1, num df = 1588, denom df = 1564, p-value = 0.3
#> alternative hypothesis: true ratio of variances is not equal to 1
#> 95 percent confidence interval:
#>  0.9527 1.1608
#> sample estimates:
#> ratio of variances 
#>              1.052
leveneTest(weight ~ dibpat, data = wcgs)         # Levene's test (robust to non-normality)
#> Levene's Test for Homogeneity of Variance (center = median)
#>         Df F value Pr(>F)
#> group    1    0.36   0.55
#>       3152

On the full cohort, both the \(F\)-test (\(p=0.32\)) and Levene’s test (\(p=0.55\)) agree the weight variances in Type A and Type B do not differ significantly — confirming that our small subsample’s apparent \(20\)-fold variance difference above was simply sampling noise, and that Student’s and Welch’s tests should (and, as we will see, do) give essentially the same answer on the full data.

Mann-Whitney U Test (Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test)

When to use it

Use the Mann-Whitney U test (equivalently, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test — the same procedure under two names) instead of a \(t\)-test when:

  • The outcome is ordinal, or numeric but clearly non-normal / skewed in one or both groups.
  • There are influential outliers.
  • Sample sizes are small and unequal, compounding concerns about non-normality.

It tests whether observations from one group tend to be systematically larger than observations from the other (a shift in location/distribution), without assuming a particular distributional shape.

\[ H_0: \text{the two populations have the same distribution (no location shift)} \]

Procedure

  1. Combine the two samples and rank all \(n_1+n_2\) values together from smallest (rank 1) to largest, giving tied values the average of the ranks they span.
  2. Sum the ranks in each group: \(R_1\) (group 1) and \(R_2\) (group 2); note \(R_1+R_2=\dfrac{(n_1+n_2)(n_1+n_2+1)}{2}\).
  3. Convert each rank sum to a \(U\) statistic: \[ U_1=R_1-\frac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2}, \qquad U_2=R_2-\frac{n_2(n_2+1)}{2} \tag{25.1}\] and note \(U_1+U_2=n_1n_2\) always.
  4. The test statistic is conventionally \(U=\min(U_1,U_2)\) (R’s wilcox.test() reports \(W\), which equals \(U\) for the first-listed group).
  5. For small samples, compare \(U\) to exact tables; for larger samples, use the normal approximation: \[ z=\frac{U-\mu_U}{\sigma_U}, \qquad \mu_U=\frac{n_1n_2}{2}, \qquad \sigma_U=\sqrt{\frac{n_1n_2(n_1+n_2+1)}{12}} \tag{25.2}\] (software also applies a tie correction to \(\sigma_U\) and often a continuity correction).

#>

#> W = 43, p-value = 0.3
#> alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

#>  Wilcoxon rank sum test
#> 
#> data:  g1 and g2
#> W = 43, p-value = 0.2
#> alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

R’s \(W=43\) is exactly our \(U_1\) (the “Yes” group is the first argument). Without the continuity correction, \(p=0.248\) matches our hand calculation; with it (R’s default), \(p=0.270\). Neither reaches significance in this small subsample — but note the extreme value (\(645\)) did not distort the result, because the Mann-Whitney test uses only its rank (the largest), unlike a \(t\)-test where it would inflate the group variance and mean substantially.

A Practical Decision Process

  1. Plot the data (boxplots, histograms) for each group.
  2. Check normality within each group (Q-Q plots; Shapiro-Wilk for smaller \(n\), interpreted cautiously for large \(n\)).
  3. Check variance homogeneity (Levene’s test is generally preferred over the \(F\)-test, since it is far less sensitive to non-normality).
  4. Choose accordingly:
Normality? Equal variances? Recommended test
Yes Yes Student’s (pooled-variance) \(t\)-test
Yes No Welch’s \(t\)-test
No (or small, skewed, or outlier-prone) — Mann-Whitney U test

Many statisticians now recommend simply defaulting to Welch’s test for two-group comparisons (it is R’s default), since it costs almost nothing when variances are equal and protects against the inflated false-positive rate of Student’s test when they are not.

Common Pitfalls

  • Assuming equal variances without checking. Especially with unequal group sizes, ignoring unequal variances (using Student’s test when Welch’s is warranted) can distort the Type I error rate — the larger group’s variance can dominate misleadingly.
  • Using the \(F\)-test to check variance equality on non-normal data. As seen above, the \(F\)-test can itself mislead when the data are skewed; prefer Levene’s test.
  • Over-relying on Shapiro-Wilk in large samples. With \(n\) in the thousands, trivial deviations from normality become “significant” — always inspect the plot as well.
  • Letting outliers dominate a \(t\)-test. A single extreme value (like the \(645\) mg/dL cholesterol reading here) can materially shift a group’s mean and variance; the Mann-Whitney test is far more robust to this.
  • Interpreting the Mann-Whitney test as strictly a median comparison. It formally tests for a general distributional/location shift; it approximates a median comparison well only when the two distributions have similar shape and spread.
  • Confusing statistical and clinical significance. With WCGS’s large \(n\), even modest mean differences (e.g., \(1.8\) lb in weight) become statistically significant — always report and interpret the effect size and CI, not just the \(p\)-value.

Summary

  • To compare the means of two independent groups, first assess normality and variance homogeneity within each group.
  • Student’s pooled-variance \(t\)-test is appropriate when both groups are approximately normal with similar variances (as with weight by behaviour pattern in the WCGS data: \(t=2.39\), \(p=0.017\), essentially identical to Welch’s result).
  • Welch’s \(t\)-test relaxes the equal-variance assumption and is a safe general default; it diverges most from Student’s test when variances and sample sizes are both unequal (as with cholesterol by CHD status: Student’s \(df=3140\) vs. Welch’s \(df=290.3\), though both still reach the same substantive conclusion here).
  • The Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) compares two groups using ranks rather than raw values, requiring no distributional assumption beyond independence — it is the method of choice for skewed data, small samples, or data with outliers, and agreed closely with the parametric tests in our cholesterol example (Hodges-Lehmann shift \(24.0\) mg/dL vs. mean difference \(25.8\) mg/dL).
